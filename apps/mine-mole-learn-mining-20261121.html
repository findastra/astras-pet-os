1. What a cryptocurrency is

Picture a notebook that says who paid whom: "Astra paid Bob 2 coins." A bank normally keeps that notebook. A cryptocurrency has no bank: thousands of computers around the world each keep their own copy, and they agree on every new page.

Each page is called a block, and the pages are stapled in order into a blockchain. The hard part is deciding who gets to write the next page, without anyone being in charge. Mining is the answer: computers race to solve a puzzle, and the winner writes the page and is paid in new coins for it.

Standard terms: distributed ledger, consensus. Bitcoin's way of doing it is called proof of work.

2. A hash: a fingerprint for any data

A hash function takes any data, a word or a whole book, and gives back a fixed-size fingerprint. Bitcoin uses SHA-256, whose fingerprint is 64 characters of 0–9 and a–f.

  • The same input always gives the same fingerprint.
  • Change one letter and the fingerprint changes completely; you can't predict how.
  • You can't run it backwards: the fingerprint doesn't tell you the input.

Try it in hash-lab-tool on this page: type "hello", then "Hello", and compare.

3. The puzzle: proof of work

A block holds the new payments, the fingerprint of the page before it (that's the staple), and one extra number called the nonce ("number used once"). The puzzle: find a nonce that makes the block's fingerprint start with enough zeros.

Because you can't run a hash backwards, there is no clever shortcut. You guess a nonce, hash, check, and guess again. Your description was right that it's slow, but it isn't solved by thinking. It's a lottery where each guess is one ticket. Each extra zero bit required halves the chance a guess wins.

Variables

  • b = how many zero bits the fingerprint must start with (the difficulty), a count
  • E = how many guesses it takes on average, a count
  • h = your hash rate: guesses per second, in H/s
  • t = average time to win, in seconds

Equations

E = 2b

t = E ÷ h

Real scenario: this browser guesses about 400,000 times a second. How long for 20 zero bits?

  1. E = 220 = 1,048,576 guesses
  2. t = 1,048,576 ÷ 400,000 H/s
  3. t = 2.6 seconds on average

So mining-operation-tool finds a ConceptCoin block every few seconds at 20 bits. Some blocks take ten times longer and some come almost at once: that's luck, and it's normal.

4. Hash rate: how fast you guess

Hash rate is guesses per second. Each unit is 1,000 times the one before:

UnitGuesses per secondExample
kH/s1,000a laptop CPU on Monero: about 3–5 kH/s
MH/s1,000,000this browser on ConceptCoin: about 0.4 MH/s
TH/s1012one Bitcoin ASIC machine: about 200–270 TH/s
EH/s1018the whole Bitcoin network: about 1,000 EH/s (late 2026, approx.)
ZH/s10211,000 EH/s = 1 ZH/s

The coin you mine decides which hash rate matters: your hash rate on that coin's algorithm, compared with everyone else's on the same coin.

5. Difficulty adjusts itself

If more miners join, blocks would come faster. So the network checks how long recent blocks took and makes the puzzle harder or easier to stay on schedule. It works like a thermostat: a feedback control loop. Bitcoin aims for one block every 10 minutes and adjusts every 2,016 blocks (about two weeks).

Variables

  • Dold = the difficulty now, a number
  • Ttarget = how long the last 2,016 blocks should have taken: 14 days
  • Tactual = how long they really took, in days
  • Dnew = the difficulty after the adjustment

Equation

Dnew = Dold × (Ttarget ÷ Tactual)

Real scenario: new miners joined and the last 2,016 blocks took only 12 days.

  1. Dnew = Dold × (14 ÷ 12)
  2. Dnew = Dold × 1.167

The puzzle gets about 17% harder, so everyone's share of the rewards shrinks. Watch the same thing happen every few blocks in mining-operation-tool.

6. The reward, and halvings

The winner of each block gets newly made coins (the block subsidy) plus the fees people paid to send their payments. Bitcoin's subsidy halves every 210,000 blocks, about every four years: it has been 3.125 BTC since April 2024 and drops to 1.5625 BTC around 2028. That's why mining gets harder to profit from over time unless the price rises.

Not every coin does this. Monero pays a steady 0.6 XMR a block forever (its "tail emission"), with a block about every 2 minutes. ConceptCoin halves every 8 blocks so you can watch it happen.

7. Mining alone vs in a pool

Solo: you keep the whole reward, but only when you win the block yourself. Pool: thousands of miners work together and split each reward by how much work each one sent. You prove your work with shares: near-misses that are much easier to find than a real block. Pools turn a lottery into a small, steady wage, minus a fee (often 1–2%).

Variables

  • h = your hash rate, in H/s
  • H = the whole network's hash rate, in H/s
  • R = coins paid per block
  • B = blocks found per day by the whole network (Bitcoin: 144)
  • f = the pool's fee, as a fraction (1% = 0.01)
  • T = average days until you win a block solo
  • D = coins you earn per day in a pool

Equations

T = (H ÷ h) ÷ B

D = (h ÷ H) × R × B × (1 − f)

Real scenario: one 200 TH/s Bitcoin machine, network 1,000 EH/s, reward 3.125 BTC, no fee.

  1. H ÷ h = 1,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 ÷ 200,000,000,000,000 = 5,000,000
  2. T = 5,000,000 ÷ 144 = 34,722 days: about 95 years to win one block alone
  3. D = (1 ÷ 5,000,000) × 3.125 × 144 × 1 = 0.00009 BTC a day in a pool

Same machine, same expected coins over a lifetime: the pool just pays you a little every day instead of maybe once.

8. Processor, RAM and which machine fits which coin

Each coin picks its own hashing recipe (its algorithm), and the recipe decides the best machine:

MachineWhat it isGood for
CPUyour computer's main processor: a few fast, flexible coresMonero (RandomX), which was designed so CPUs stay competitive
GPUa graphics card: thousands of small cores working in parallelGPU coins such as Ravencoin or Ergo; Ethereum stopped mining in 2022
ASICa chip built to do one algorithm only, nothing elseBitcoin (SHA-256); once ASICs exist for a coin, CPUs and GPUs can't compete

RAM: Bitcoin doesn't care. Monero's RandomX needs about 2–3 GB of free RAM for its fast mode, and about 2 MB of the CPU's L3 cache per mining thread, so cache size often limits you more than core count. Dual-channel memory and "huge pages" can add 20–50%. GPU coins that are "memory-hard" care about the card's VRAM size and speed.

Mine Mole's weekly check needs your graphics card model and your power price to say which of these fits you.

9. Electricity: the cost that decides everything

Variables

  • P = the machine's power draw, in watts (W)
  • 24 = hours in a day
  • 1000 = watts in a kilowatt
  • p = your electricity price, in dollars per kilowatt-hour ($/kWh), from your power bill
  • C = power cost per day, in dollars
  • e = efficiency: joules spent per terahash (J/TH); lower is better
  • hTH = the machine's hash rate in TH/s

Equations

C = P × 24 ÷ 1000 × p

e = P ÷ hTH

Real scenario: a 3,500 W, 200 TH/s Bitcoin machine at $0.15 per kWh.

  1. C = 3,500 × 24 ÷ 1,000 × 0.15
  2. C = 84 kWh × $0.15 = $12.60 a day
  3. e = 3,500 W ÷ 200 TH/s = 17.5 J/TH

Professional mines pay $0.03–0.06 per kWh, so the same machine costs them a third as much to run. That's why home Bitcoin mining usually loses money. The newest machines reach about 13–17 J/TH; older ones above 30 J/TH rarely pay.

10. Cooling: every watt becomes heat

A miner turns almost all the electricity it uses into heat, so a mining computer is also a heater.

Variables

  • P = power draw, in watts
  • 3.412 = BTU per hour that one watt gives off as heat
  • Q = heat given off, in BTU per hour (the unit heaters and air conditioners use)

Equation

Q = P × 3.412

Real scenario: the 3,500 W machine from lesson 9.

  1. Q = 3,500 × 3.412
  2. Q = 11,942 BTU per hour, as much as two 1,500 W space heaters and a bit more
  • ASICs are loud, 75–80 dB, like a vacuum cleaner: most live in a garage or shed.
  • Keep CPUs and GPUs under about 80 °C. Laptops aren't built for 100% all day: they slow down and wear out.
  • In winter the heat can warm a room, which gives back part of the power cost.
11. Is it worth the power?

Variables

  • D = coins earned per day (lesson 7)
  • v = the price of one coin, in dollars
  • C = power cost per day, in dollars (lesson 9)
  • N = profit per day, in dollars: below 0 means you lose money

Equation

N = D × v − C

Real scenario: a laptop mining Monero at 4 kH/s, network 5 GH/s, 0.6 XMR × 720 blocks a day, an example price of $300 per XMR, and 65 W more power at $0.15 per kWh.

  1. D = (4,000 ÷ 5,000,000,000) × 0.6 × 720 = 0.0003456 XMR a day
  2. D × v = 0.0003456 × $300 = $0.104
  3. C = 65 × 24 ÷ 1,000 × 0.15 = $0.234
  4. N = $0.104 − $0.234 = −$0.13 a day

The laptop loses about 13 cents a day, before wear. Put your own numbers in worth-the-power-tool.

12. Staying safe, and the law
  • "Cloud mining" contracts that promise daily returns are almost always scams or lose money.
  • Never mine on someone else's computer: work, school or a cloud server. It breaks their rules, and secretly mining on other people's machines ("cryptojacking") is what malware does.
  • Download miners only from their official project page; antivirus often flags real miners too, so check the checksum the project publishes.
  • Wallets: your coins are controlled by a secret "seed phrase" of 12–24 words. Anyone who sees it owns your coins. Never type it into a website or a chat, an AI chat included.
  • Taxes: in the US and many other countries, mined coins count as income at their value on the day you get them.
  • These lessons are education, not financial advice.
